SHOCKnumbers that don't compute

170GW
Igniting Saturn V’s five F-1s released chemical energy at roughly 170 GW — about 80 times the peak output of the Hoover Dam, concentrated in a 110 m tube.
Apollo · en.wikipedia.org
12.9kkg/s
The five F-1 engines together drank 12,890 kg of propellant every second — 4,880 litres of kerosene alone, enough to fill an Olympic swimming pool in eight and a half minutes.
Apollo · en.wikipedia.org
2.2kt
Saturn V’s 42 m first stage stood on the pad at 2,214 tonnes fully fuelled — and was drained completely dry in 165 seconds.
Apollo · en.wikipedia.org
81MN
Starship’s 33 Raptors produce 80.8 MN at liftoff — more than twice Saturn V’s 34.5 MN. It is the most power ever controlled by a human-made machine.
New Space · en.wikipedia.org
8km/s
To stay in orbit a rocket must reach 7.8 km/s — Mach 23, about 28,000 km/h — and it has roughly eight minutes to do it from a standing start.
Physics · en.wikipedia.org
20×
The rocket equation made flesh: Saturn V stood 2,822 tonnes on the pad to deliver 140 tonnes to orbit — roughly 20 kg of rocket and propellant for every 1 kg of payload.
Physics · en.wikipedia.org
37flights
A single Falcon 9 booster has now flown 37 missions — a machine that cost tens of millions of dollars, reused like an airliner.
New Space · en.wikipedia.org
9days
Falcon 9 booster B1088 flew twice just 9 days, 3 hours and 39 minutes apart — the fastest turnaround of any orbital-class booster ever.
New Space · en.wikipedia.org
16.0kactive
Around 16,000 functioning satellites now orbit Earth, of which roughly 11,150 belong to a single constellation: Starlink.
Now · aerospaceglobalnews.com
1.0ksats
Falcon 9 booster B1071 became the first to launch 1,000 cumulative spacecraft — a single first stage has now dispensed more satellites than most whole space programmes.
New Space · en.wikipedia.org
39.9kkm/h
The fastest humans ever travelled were the Apollo 10 crew, re-entering at 39,897 km/h — a record unbroken for 57 years.
Apollo · en.wikipedia.org
192km/s
Parker Solar Probe is the fastest object ever built: 191.7 km/s relative to the Sun, fast enough to cross the Atlantic in 28 seconds.
Now · en.wikipedia.org
59.7k$/kg
The Space Shuttle cost about $59,700 to put one kilogramme in orbit. A reusable Falcon 9 lists at roughly $3,250 — an eighteenfold collapse in the price of escape.
Shuttle → New Space · en.wikipedia.org
9.0kdays
Humans have lived continuously off Earth since 2 November 2000 — more than 9,000 unbroken days of occupation in orbit.
ISS Era · en.wikipedia.org
1.1klaunches
The Soyuz rocket family has flown more than 1,150 times — nobody has ever built a more reliable machine for leaving the planet.
Workhorses · en.wikipedia.org
257.0khp
The RD-170’s single turbopump delivers 257,000 hp to four combustion chambers at once — the power of three nuclear icebreakers in one machine.
Soviet · en.wikipedia.org
14.7kkN
One Shuttle solid booster pushed out 14,700 kN at liftoff — about 29 times the thrust of a GE90, the most powerful jet engine ever built.
Shuttle · en.wikipedia.org
1.1ML
Every Shuttle launch sprayed 1.1 million litres of water under the stack at T-16 s — without it, the SRBs’ acoustic energy would have shaken the orbiter to pieces.
Shuttle · en.wikipedia.org
1.4k°C
A Starship re-entering at orbital speed generates plasma near 1,400 °C — hot enough to vaporise aluminium, deflected by 18,000 hexagonal silica tiles.
Physics · en.wikipedia.org
16km/s
New Horizons left Earth at 16.26 km/s — the fastest departure ever, crossing the Moon’s orbit in nine hours on a trip that took Apollo crews three days.
Golden Age · en.wikipedia.org
2.6km/s
An F-1’s exhaust leaves the nozzle at about 2,580 m/s — nearly three times the muzzle velocity of a 5.56 mm rifle bullet.
Apollo · en.wikipedia.org
